Emergency drama

PILOT 01 · EMERGENCY

Trauma Bay

THE PITCH · READ THIS BEFORE RATING

One night. One overcrowded emergency department. No reset button. Trauma Bay follows the same clinical team through a single overnight shift, where three unstable patients can arrive before the first case is resolved. The drama comes from prioritising, communicating under pressure and deciding what can safely wait.

Pilot episode: a 28-year-old cyclist arrives after a road collision. His blood pressure starts to fall and his abdominal pain worsens. A junior doctor suspects internal bleeding while the consultant activates the trauma team and orders urgent assessment. The episode becomes more dramatic when the junior doctor then appears to follow the patient through scanning, theatre, family updates and discharge planning almost alone.

PILOT 02 · MYSTERY

The Diagnosis

THE PITCH · READ THIS BEFORE RATING

Every episode begins with a patient nobody can explain. The Diagnosis is a medical mystery series built around a prestigious diagnostic unit. The team argues through competing hypotheses, searches for overlooked clues and tries to reach an answer before the patient deteriorates.

Pilot episode: a 34-year-old teacher has recurrent abdominal pain, tingling, confusion and several previous admissions. Dr Vale spots one unusual clue and announces a rare metabolic diagnosis before the full examination and confirmatory tests are complete. A junior colleague challenges his certainty, the patient hears the disagreement, and the episode turns the clinical uncertainty into a high-stakes intellectual duel. The writers want the audience to admire Vale — but they also want us to wonder whether his certainty is credible.

Hospital ensemble

PILOT 03 · WARD LIFE

Ward 17

THE PITCH · READ THIS BEFORE RATING

The biggest decisions are not always made in resus. Ward 17 is an ensemble drama about the ordinary work that keeps a hospital running: handovers, observations, medication, discharge planning, difficult conversations, phone calls and the constant risk that a routine day becomes urgent.

Pilot episode: an older patient recovering from pneumonia is medically improving but still cannot walk safely enough to go home. The team must coordinate nursing observations, mobility, medication, family expectations and discharge support while another patient begins to deteriorate. A junior doctor has to explain uncertainty to the patient’s daughter without promising a discharge time. The tension comes from whether the team can make a safe plan before the evening handover — not from a miracle cure.

Surgical drama

PILOT 04 · SURGERY

Under the Knife

THE PITCH · READ THIS BEFORE RATING

Every operation starts long before the first incision. Under the Knife follows an elite surgical unit where ambition, hierarchy and responsibility collide. Each episode moves from consultation and consent to theatre and post-operative care, showing how a difficult operation affects both the patient and the team.

Pilot episode: surgeon Maya Chen recommends a complex abdominal operation and tells the patient that surgery may offer the best option but cannot guarantee the outcome. In theatre, a complication forces the team to change plan. The scenes show an anaesthetist, scrub team and assistants — yet the marketing repeatedly presents Maya as the lone surgeon who “saves impossible cases”. After the operation, a junior colleague worries that the patient may have heard the benefits more clearly than the risks.

What TV Leaves Out

A 45-minute episode cannot show an entire shift. Your job is to identify the ordinary work that disappears off screen — and to distinguish sensible compression from a distorted picture of medicine.

THE INVISIBLE SHIFT

Medicine does not stop when the camera cuts.

The evidence desk highlights ordinary work that television rarely foregrounds.

📝 charting☎️ phone calls🤝 patient advocacy👥 team care⏳ waiting📋 handovers📈 routine monitoring🔁 follow-up
Two evidence signals

Cleveland Clinic: medical dramas often simplify team roles and leave ordinary work such as patient advocacy, filling in charts and making phone calls in the background.

Witten/Herdecke research: after analysing more than 300 episodes, the project found that illness, death and intensive-care scenarios can differ sharply from reality; television often foregrounds dramatic emergency operations while real death may be quiet.

This mission reviews representation and storytelling. It is not clinical training.

WRITERS’ ROOM · MISSION 06

Notes for the Writers

The platform has the ratings. Now make them useful. For each scene, decide whether the production should KEEP, CHANGE, CUT or ADD CONTEXT — then phrase the note like a professional medical consultant.

WRITER NOTES0 / 20
KEEPAccurate or useful drama. Preserve it.
CHANGEThe idea can stay, but the execution needs revision.
CUTThe element actively misleads and adds little value.
ADD CONTEXTKeep the dramatic beat, but restore missing clinical reality.
01Protect the drama

Do not sterilise the script. Medical realism and entertainment can coexist.

02Name the problem

Timing? Teamwork? Communication? Ethics? Missing uncertainty?

03Give an actionable note

Tell the writers what to change, not merely that something feels wrong.

04Use consultant language

“Consider…”, “Could we…?”, “Avoid implying…”, “This may work better if…”
